# Semantic Alignment, Meaning Preservation, and Reality Drift

## Why Systems Can Remain Coherent While Meaning Gradually Changes

AI Systems and Reality Drift Note #9  
*A. Jacobs* - Semantic Fidelity Lab

## The Basic Pattern

Modern information systems depend on the ability to preserve meaning across transformations.

Information is summarized.

Translated.

Compressed.

Stored.

Retrieved.

Transmitted.

Interpreted.

Generated.

At each stage, representations are created in order to make information easier to process.

Most systems assume that meaning survives these transformations.

A translated sentence is expected to preserve the original meaning.

A summary is expected to preserve the important ideas.

A retrieval system is expected to preserve the relationship between a source and its interpretation.

An AI system is expected to preserve the intent contained within an instruction.

At first this assumption appears reasonable.

The outputs remain coherent.

The language remains fluent.

The information appears recognizable.

Yet a familiar pattern often begins to emerge.

Small changes accumulate.

Interpretations drift.

Definitions shift.

Context disappears.

Meaning gradually changes while coherence remains intact.

Different communities describe these failures using different terminology:

- semantic similarity
- semantic consistency
- semantic alignment
- semantic preservation
- meaning preservation
- semantic equivalence
- semantic coherence
- semantic integrity
- semantic accuracy
- semantic grounding

Although these concepts emphasize different aspects of language and information systems, they often point toward the same underlying challenge.

How can meaning remain stable as information passes through representations?

## When Coherence Replaces Meaning

Many systems evaluate information using surface indicators.

Outputs are measured for fluency.

Responses are measured for coherence.

Summaries are measured for readability.

Translations are measured for similarity.

Embeddings are measured for proximity.

These measurements are useful.

But they are not identical to meaning.

A statement can remain coherent while changing its meaning.

A summary can remain accurate while omitting critical context.

A translation can remain grammatically correct while altering intent.

An AI response can remain plausible while drifting away from the information it was meant to represent.

The challenge is that coherence is often easier to measure than meaning.

As a result, systems frequently optimize for coherence while assuming meaning remains preserved.

## Related Concepts Across Fields

Different disciplines approach this problem through different terminology.

Natural language processing researchers often discuss semantic similarity, measuring whether two pieces of text express related meanings.

Researchers evaluating language models increasingly examine semantic consistency, asking whether information remains stable across multiple outputs.

Alignment researchers focus on semantic alignment, examining whether models preserve intended meaning when interpreting instructions.

Translation researchers frequently study meaning preservation and semantic equivalence, asking whether transformed information retains its original intent.

Knowledge representation systems often emphasize semantic integrity, ensuring information remains internally consistent as it moves between systems.

Researchers evaluating AI reliability increasingly discuss semantic grounding, focusing on whether meaning remains anchored to evidence, context, or reality.

Although the language differs, these concepts often point toward the same structural problem:

Representations remain coherent while meaning gradually changes.

## How Semantic Drift Emerges

The shift from meaning preservation to semantic drift typically unfolds in several stages.

### Stage 1 — Representation

Information is encoded into a representation.

Meaning remains closely connected to the original source.

### Stage 2 — Transformation

The representation is translated, summarized, compressed, stored, retrieved, interpreted, or generated.

The information remains recognizable.

### Stage 3 — Accumulation

Additional transformations occur.

Context is removed.

Assumptions are introduced.

Information becomes increasingly dependent upon intermediate representations.

### Stage 4 — Drift

The representation remains coherent.

The language remains fluent.

The structure remains intact.

Yet the relationship between the representation and the original meaning gradually weakens.

The system continues functioning while semantic fidelity declines.

## Examples Across Systems

### Translation Systems

A translation may preserve most words and concepts while subtly altering intent, emphasis, or context.

The text remains understandable.

The meaning changes.

### Summarization Systems

A summary may accurately capture major points while removing important qualifications or constraints.

The information remains coherent.

The original meaning becomes narrower.

### Knowledge Retrieval

Retrieved information may remain factually correct while being interpreted outside the context that originally gave it meaning.

The facts survive.

The significance changes.

### Language Models

A model may generate responses that appear semantically plausible while introducing subtle distortions not present in the source material.

The output remains coherent.

The relationship to the original meaning weakens.

## Semantic Alignment and Reality Drift

Within the Reality Drift framework, semantic alignment describes the challenge of maintaining meaning across representations.

Information systems depend upon transformations.

Representations are unavoidable.

Compression is unavoidable.

Abstraction is unavoidable.

The challenge is not eliminating representations.

The challenge is preserving meaning as information passes through them.

As systems scale, optimize, and automate these transformations, the possibility of semantic drift increases.

Representations remain active.

Communication continues.

Outputs remain coherent.

Yet meaning may gradually become detached from the realities those representations were intended to describe.

## Recognizing the Pattern

Semantic drift often goes unnoticed because coherence remains visible.

The sentence still makes sense.

The summary still appears accurate.

The response still sounds reasonable.

The translation still reads correctly.

Observers therefore assume meaning has been preserved.

But meaning can change long before coherence fails.

This creates a familiar paradox:

The representation appears increasingly coherent while becoming progressively less faithful to the meaning it was meant to preserve.

Understanding semantic alignment, semantic preservation, semantic integrity, and semantic grounding helps explain why information systems can continue functioning successfully while gradually losing contact with the meanings they were designed to transmit.

## Meaning and Representation

Information systems do not move meaning directly.

They move representations of meaning.

Words, symbols, embeddings, summaries, translations, databases, and model outputs all function as representations of underlying concepts.

These representations allow information to scale across people, organizations, and machines.

But every transformation introduces the possibility of semantic loss.

The challenge is not simply transmitting information.

The challenge is maintaining fidelity between representations and the meanings they were intended to preserve.

This is the deeper connection between semantic similarity, semantic alignment, semantic integrity, semantic grounding, and meaning preservation.

The failure is not necessarily incorrect information.

The failure is the gradual weakening of the relationship between representation and meaning.
