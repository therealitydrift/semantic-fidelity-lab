# Performance Metrics, KPI Distortion, and Campbell's Law

## Why Indicators Stop Reflecting the Realities They Were Built to Measure

AI Systems and Reality Drift Note #6 
*A. Jacobs* - Semantic Fidelity Lab

## The Basic Pattern

Modern organizations depend on measurement.

Governments track social outcomes through statistical indicators.

Schools evaluate performance through testing systems.

Companies manage operations through dashboards and KPIs.

Healthcare systems monitor quality through performance metrics.

Large institutions cannot directly observe every outcome they care about.

Instead, they rely on indicators that summarize complex realities into measurable signals.

Examples include:

- graduation rates as a proxy for educational quality
- performance metrics as a proxy for productivity
- response times as a proxy for service quality
- test scores as a proxy for learning
- KPIs as a proxy for organizational effectiveness

At first these indicators serve an important purpose.

They provide visibility.

They improve coordination.

They help organizations make decisions.

But over time a familiar pattern often begins to emerge.

The indicators become increasingly important.

Funding depends on them.

Promotions depend on them.

Reputation depends on them.

Evaluation depends on them.

As pressure increases, people begin optimizing the measurement itself.

The indicator remains visible.

The underlying reality becomes harder to see.

## When Measurement Becomes the Target

Indicators are designed to represent conditions that are difficult to observe directly.

The measurement is not the outcome.

The measurement is a representation of the outcome.

At first the relationship remains relatively stable.

Higher test scores generally correspond to more learning.

Higher productivity metrics generally correspond to greater output.

Better performance indicators generally correspond to better results.

But as indicators become increasingly important, incentives begin changing.

People learn how the measurement works.

Organizations learn what the evaluation rewards.

Systems adapt to the metric.

Optimization pressure concentrates around the representation.

The measurement becomes the target.

Once this occurs, improvement in the indicator no longer guarantees improvement in the underlying reality.

The metric continues rising.

The outcome may not.

## Related Concepts Across Fields

Different disciplines describe this pattern using different terminology.

Social scientist Donald Campbell described the phenomenon through Campbell's Law, which states that the more a quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more subject it becomes to corruption pressures and the more likely it is to distort the processes it is intended to monitor.

Economists often describe a similar dynamic through Goodhart's Law, commonly summarized as:

> When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Management researchers discuss KPI distortion, metric gaming, and target corruption, where performance indicators are optimized without improving real outcomes.

Education researchers examine teaching to the test, accountability systems, and performance measurement distortions.

Public policy researchers study indicator manipulation, performance management failures, and outcome measurement problems.

Although the language differs, these concepts often point toward the same structural pattern:

Optimization increasingly targets the measurement rather than the reality the measurement was designed to represent.

## How Indicator Corruption Emerges

The shift from measurement to distortion typically unfolds in several stages.

### Stage 1 — Observation

An organization introduces a measurement system.

The indicator remains reasonably connected to the underlying condition.

The measurement functions as a useful signal.

### Stage 2 — Incentivization

The measurement becomes tied to funding, rewards, accountability, status, or performance evaluation.

Improving the metric becomes increasingly valuable.

### Stage 3 — Optimization

Individuals and organizations adapt their behavior to improve the indicator.

Effort increasingly focuses on the measurement itself.

Strategies emerge that improve the metric more efficiently than improving the underlying outcome.

### Stage 4 — Indicator Corruption

The measurement continues improving.

The dashboard continues showing success.

The reported performance continues rising.

Yet the relationship between the indicator and the reality it represents begins weakening.

The representation remains healthy.

The reality becomes uncertain.

## Examples Across Systems

### Education

Standardized testing systems are introduced to measure learning outcomes.

As accountability increases, schools increasingly focus on improving test performance.

Scores improve.

Learning may not improve at the same rate.

The indicator becomes more important than the condition it was intended to measure.

### Corporate Performance Metrics

Organizations use KPIs to monitor productivity and effectiveness.

Employees and departments learn how performance is evaluated.

Effort increasingly focuses on improving measured indicators.

The dashboard improves.

Actual performance becomes more difficult to evaluate.

### Healthcare Systems

Healthcare organizations often rely on measurable quality indicators.

As reporting requirements expand, attention increasingly focuses on documented performance rather than unmeasured aspects of patient care.

The metrics improve.

The broader outcome becomes harder to assess.

### Public Policy

Governments frequently use statistical indicators to evaluate programs and institutions.

As these indicators become tied to funding and accountability, organizations optimize for the measurement itself.

The reported outcome improves.

The underlying problem may remain unchanged.

## Campbell's Law and Reality Drift

Within the Reality Drift framework, Campbell's Law describes one of the most common mechanisms through which organizations lose contact with reality.

The system does not stop measuring.

The dashboard does not disappear.

The reports do not become empty.

In many cases, measurement activity actually increases.

More metrics are collected.

More indicators are monitored.

More reporting occurs.

Yet the relationship between those indicators and the realities they were designed to represent gradually weakens.

The organization continues functioning.

The measurement system continues functioning.

The drift occurs within the relationship between the representation and the reality.

## Recognizing the Pattern

Indicator corruption often goes unnoticed because the measurements continue suggesting success.

Performance indicators improve.

Targets are achieved.

Reports remain positive.

Dashboards remain green.

Observers therefore conclude that the underlying system is improving.

Yet important conditions may remain unchanged or even deteriorate.

This creates a familiar paradox:

The system appears increasingly successful according to its indicators while becoming progressively less certain about the realities those indicators were meant to measure.

Understanding Campbell's Law, KPI distortion, metric gaming, and target corruption helps explain why organizations can become increasingly measurement-driven while becoming less connected to the outcomes those measurements were intended to improve.

## Measurement and Representation

Organizations cannot directly manage every aspect of reality.

They rely on representations.

Metrics, indicators, dashboards, KPIs, performance reports, rankings, and evaluation systems all function as representations of broader conditions.

These representations make large-scale coordination possible.

But every representation introduces the possibility of distortion.

When organizations increasingly optimize the representation itself, the indicator gradually loses its ability to faithfully reflect reality.

This is the deeper connection between Campbell's Law, Goodhart's Law, KPI distortion, metric gaming, and target corruption.

The challenge is not measurement.

The challenge is maintaining fidelity between the measurement and the reality it was designed to represent.
