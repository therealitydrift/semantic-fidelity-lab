# A Semantic Fidelity Lexicon: Core Terms for Meaning, Drift, and Decay

*A foundational glossary for understanding semantic drift, fidelity decay, and the hidden failure modes of meaning in generative systems.*

**Semantic Fidelity Project**  
**Jul 23, 2024**

In generative systems, the failure mode of language is not only error. It is also erosion. Text can remain fluent, coherent, and factually acceptable while becoming less connected to the intent, context, or situation it was meant to preserve. A retrieval system may surface relevant material while still missing the deeper relationship the source depends on.

This lexicon defines a small set of terms for describing those conditions. Its purpose is to clarify the main patterns of semantic loss that appear when language is compressed, transformed, and reused.

## Core Terms for Meaning, Drift, and Decay

## Semantic Fidelity

Semantic fidelity is the preservation of meaning, intent, context, nuance, and interpretive structure as language moves through a system. A high-fidelity output does not merely sound correct. It keeps enough of the original situation intact for the language to still do the work it was meant to do. This extends beyond factual accuracy. A statement can remain technically true while losing tone, hierarchy, implication, metaphor, or framing. Semantic fidelity names that deeper layer of preservation.

## Semantic Drift

Semantic drift is the gradual mutation of meaning across transformation. In AI systems, drift often preserves surface coherence while altering tone, emphasis, framing, or intent. The output may still look connected to the source, but the relationship has changed. This is why drift can be hard to detect. It does not always appear as a clean error. It often appears as language that still works while becoming less connected to what it originally carried.

## Fidelity Decay

Fidelity decay is the weakening of semantic integrity across repeated compression, summarization, reinterpretation, or generation. Each transformation may look acceptable on its own, but small losses accumulate over time. The danger is cumulative. A system can preserve the surface of language while gradually reducing grounding, nuance, and contextual structure. By the end, the output may still be fluent and usable, but less able to preserve the original intent.

## Ground Erosion

Ground erosion happens when the background that gives language weight begins to collapse, weakening the implied, contextual, or deliberately unsaid elements that meaning depends on. AI systems often make language more explicit. That can be useful, but it can also flatten the relation between foreground and background. A summary that turns every detail into the same kind of information may preserve content while losing hierarchy.

## Lexical Decay

Lexical decay describes the narrowing of expressive language as precise, rare, metaphorical, or context-rich terms are replaced by safer and more generic language. The word may remain legible, but its connection to concrete use has weakened. This is why some language can feel polished and empty at the same time. The system has learned fluent expression, but not necessarily the conditions that give expression weight.

## Semantic Noise

Semantic noise appears when fluent generated language saturates an information environment. The issue is whether the surrounding ecosystem becomes harder to search, interpret, or trust because too much language carries too little distinction. This becomes a corpus-level problem. Search results, summaries, and generated answers may remain readable while offering less contact with source, context, or judgment.

## Meaning Debt

Meaning debt is the accumulated cost of small semantic losses. Each compression, simplification, or shortcut may seem harmless in isolation, but over time the system builds a gap between the richness of the original context and the thinner representations that continue to circulate. Meaning debt matters because drift compounds through small losses that remain invisible until the system has become harder to trust.

## Synthetic Coherence

Synthetic coherence describes language that appears fluent, consistent, and persuasive while becoming less grounded underneath. The output holds together at the surface, but the connection to source, context, or reality has weakened. This is one of the central problems for AI evaluation. A system can sound coherent enough to pass while still failing to preserve what mattered.

## Evaluation Blindness

Evaluation blindness occurs when benchmarks or metrics confirm surface correctness while missing semantic degradation. A system may pass because the output is fluent, relevant, or factually acceptable, even though context, intent, or interpretive structure has weakened.

## Use of This Lexicon

This lexicon is a way to describe losses that often hide beneath fluent output. Some happen inside a sentence or summary. Others build up across retrieval, memory, repeated generation, and reuse. In each case, the system may still sound coherent while its connection to the original meaning has weakened.

The vocabulary separates these failures instead of treating them all as hallucination or error. It gives clearer language for where preservation failed, where evaluation missed it, and where stronger grounding is needed. That is where semantic fidelity becomes useful for evaluation. Without language for these losses, they remain difficult to observe and easier to ignore.
